Project retrospective · Flatiron Health

Replacing a legacy EHR tasking system under a contractual deadline

Led a team of 4–5 engineers (me included) over ~6–8 months and delivered a high-risk replacement on time — through constrained scope, controlled coexistence, and incremental rollout.

Andrew Ingraham · Senior Software Engineer, project lead

One-liner first: "Tasks is how staff at an oncology practice route work to each other inside the EHR — near-realtime messaging tied to patients and orders." The title already states the outcome; the deck's job is to defend how.

Stakes & constraints

Why this wasn't just a CRUD feature

  • A top-10 most requested product change, sustained over an extended period — and a high-visibility initiative internally.
  • A legacy Tasking feature that was badly broken but still actively used: the replacement had to be a functional superset, transitioned without breaking live clinical workflows.
  • A contractual obligation to deliver to a subset of practices by a fixed date, with significant business cost to a miss.
  • 3–4 engineers besides me, in one of two work-streams — the other carrying an EHR certification with its own immovable deadline, so people had to stay movable between streams.
Every design decision that follows traces back to these four constraints; name that explicitly. PREP: "what was the business cost of missing?" — only claim what you actually know about the contract terms.

Decision 1 of 3

Coexist with legacy, don't migrate

Alternatives
One-time migration of legacy tasks; or patch the legacy feature in place.
Chosen
Run both systems; each practice agreed a sunset date for legacy, enforced by feature flag: enable new → monitor legacy usage → disable legacy creation → off.
Benefit / cost
Migration risk off the critical path entirely; in exchange, dual support burden and active nudging of practices.
Revisit trigger
If sunset dates had slipped en masse, or dual support had begun eating the team, we'd have revisited a one-time conversion.
This is a controlled-coexistence strategy, not migration avoidance — say it that way. PREP: "did the sunset actually complete?" Know the true end state; if it was mid-rollout when you left, say so. Also: "why not migrate asynchronously after launch?", "did old and new tasks share an inbox?", "how did users know which to use?" — have real answers or honest ones.

Decision 2 of 3 · the one that bit us

Temporal tables: free audit, expensive history

  • Why they looked right: built-in row versioning, near-zero implementation cost, strong audit support, a familiar SQL Server capability.
  • The failure mode: one user action — reassign, add a recipient, change status, post a message — lands as independent version rows in four tables. Product history means merging four version streams into one ordered, human-readable timeline. confirm the specifics: ordering approach, worst downstream bugs
  • What I'd build today: temporal tables for compliance and forensics; a separate append-only activity log, written at mutation time, for user-facing history.

The lesson: audit history and product-facing activity history have different consumers and different semantics — one storage mechanism shouldn't serve both.

The deck's central technical lesson — take time here. Walk the concrete scenario slowly; it's what makes the lesson land. PREP: be ready for "was timestamp ordering sufficient?", "how did you attribute row changes to user-facing events?", "how did pagination of history perform?" — reconstruct as much as you can from memory beforehand.

Decision 3 of 3

Server-owned state over client-side cleverness

  • Task data was treated as server-owned: mutations completed on the server, then the affected views refreshed — the history panel refreshed on every edit. confirm refresh scope: whole inbox or affected resources?
  • The trade: modest extra network traffic for simpler consistency semantics and far less mutation logic to get wrong under deadline — the main alternative was mirroring state into Redux, which we deliberately bypassed for task data.
  • The data-fetching layer we built for this became a reusable strategy that replaced error-prone existing patterns beyond our feature.
Don't claim narrow query invalidation unless it's true — the honest version ("we refetched, and that was the point") defends fine. PREP: "how did two open sessions stay consistent?" ties back to the near-realtime mechanism TODO on slide 5; "was a server-state library like React Query available then?" — know the timeline answer. Loading/error handling: describe what the reusable fetching layer standardized, from memory.

Execution

The plan was a risk detector, not a waterfall

  • Ticketed and sequenced essentially the whole project up front — vertical slices, dependency-ordered so 4+ engineers worked in parallel without gating each other, with ownership areas matched to interest.
  • An auto-adjusting Gantt chart re-forecast the completion date as tickets closed: schedule risk surfaced weeks before it became unrecoverable, while estimates and scope kept moving underneath.
  • Add one concrete example: a place where the forecast moved and we cut or resequenced scope in response
The reframe matters: the goal was exposing the critical path and detecting risk early enough to act — not pretending the forecast was exact. What failed

The costs that were real

  • The temporal-table choice made user-facing history a tremendous headache, with a tail of downstream complexity (slide 8).
  • Embedding the feature in legacy Visual Basic pages, on top of error-prone existing data fetching, added constant drag — the reusable fetching layer was built in self-defense.
  • I delegated implementation ownership without first making the architectural decision boundary explicit; by the time I raised concerns, the engineer had already invested heavily in their design.
The third bullet is the process failure, owned as mine — not a story about a difficult engineer. PREP before telling it live: the technical disagreement itself, why both approaches were reasonable, how it was evaluated and decided, and how the engineer kept meaningful ownership afterward. If those specifics won't come back to you, keep the bullet at this level and don't go deeper. Optional extra if asked for more failures: the role→user mapping service for group sends turned out to be broken and needed fixing along the way.

Reflection

Three lessons I still use

  1. Separate audit storage from user-facing activity history — different consumers, different semantics, different designs.
  2. Define architectural decision rights before delegating implementation ownership, so disagreement surfaces as design review rather than as sunk cost.
  3. Sequence the whole project up front to expose the critical path — then treat the plan as a risk detector and keep revising scope and estimates against it.

Before starting, I interviewed several senior engineers about how they decomposed large projects, and synthesized that into the planning and design process I used here — and have since taught to other teams.
