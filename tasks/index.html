The feature

What is a task?

  • Somewhere between an email and a Jira ticket
  • Near-realtime messaging between users within a practice, organized as tasks
  • Each task has a subject, description, status, urgency, due date, an assignee, and recipients. Recipients can be individuals or a group like Nursing.
  • Tasks arrive in an inbox with search, pagination, sorting, and filtering, and users are notified when they receive one
  • A task can be linked to a patient or an order
  • Tasks are surfaced in a number of areas throughout the application, including on the pages of entities they're linked to
  • Every task keeps its full history, both for users and for auditing
Keep this under a minute; the demos that follow show all of it live. The lifecycle to narrate over the demos: create, resolve recipients, notify, collaborate, update status, history.

Stakes & constraints

Additional context

  • Highly requested: one of the top 10 most requested features across the organization for years
  • Tight timeline w/ significant consequences: a contractual obligation to deliver to a subset of practices by a fixed date, with significant business cost if we missed the deadline
  • Legacy system and competing products: a legacy Tasking feature that was badly broken but still actively used, so the replacement had to be a functional superset, transitioned without breaking live clinical workflows. Additionally, many practices had their own approach for messaging with each other.
  • Team breakdown: our team was made up of ~8 engineers divided loosely across 2 work streams. I was leading the work stream working on Tasks while the other work stream was working on meeting a major EHR certification with its own immovable deadline.
Every design decision that follows traces back to these four constraints; name that explicitly. PREP: "what was the business cost of missing?" — only claim what you actually know about the contract terms.

Strategy

Approach

With a tight timeline and little flexibility on resources, I wanted to derisk the contractually obligated work as much as possible: surface unknowns early and avoid missteps. Two things did most of that work.

  1. Ticketing almost every bit of work necessary for the most minimal MVP, and sequencing those tickets so 4+ engineers could always work in parallel without conflicts
  2. Asking 4–5 more senior engineers how they approached system design, and applying those approaches to this system's design. I ended up copying one engineer's approach and it's something I still follow to this day and have introduced to multiple teams since.

Alongside that, I tried to align each engineer's area of ownership with their interests

Scope

Where we drew the MVP line

MVP

  • Full task fields: subject, description, status, urgency, due date, assignee, recipients
  • Task history, for both user-friendly display and auditing
  • Notifications when a task is received
  • Inbox with search, pagination, sorting, and filtering
  • Patient and order associations, surfaced on those entities
  • Group sends (e.g. Nursing)

Permitted to come after MVP

  • Bulk task edits
  • Integrated frontend state, instead of refetching on change

  • Table indexes
  • Proper real-time notifications
  • Migrating new frontend components back into the design system
  • Styling improvements, of which there were many

Say aloud while pointing: the assignee owns the task, recipients see and follow it; groups like Nursing resolve to individual recipients at send time via the role-to-user mapping (confirm what happened when membership changed later). Notifications lifecycle to narrate: a row per recipient on new activity, row-existence meaning "unread," and (best recollection) cleared for that user and task on read — with the temporal table preserving even deleted rows for audit. The clear-on-read part is remembered; the insert side is inferred, so present it as "the shape of the design." If asked "why a separate table from Recipients": Recipients is durable membership, Notifications is transient signaling. "What happens to tasks when a user is deactivated?" — confirmed: a cronjob swept for old and orphaned tasks; a task could be orphaned when everyone associated with it had left the organization. Good edge-case story: it shows the design anticipated organizational churn, not just happy-path messaging. Concurrency (confirmed): multi-table writes wrapped in one transaction (no partial states), and optimistic concurrency on edits — the update failed if the task had changed since it was read, and the client refetched, which is the same code path as refresh-on-change. The transaction gave atomicity; the stale-read rejection prevented lost updates — name them as solving different problems. Messages were append-only, so the thread had no edit contention. Still unremembered: the token (rowversion vs. updatedAt) — "I don't recall which" is fine — and whether notification inserts were deduped by a unique (taskId, recipientId) constraint.

Decision 1 of 3

Coexist with legacy, don't migrate

Alternatives
One-time migration of legacy tasks; or patch the legacy feature in place.
Chosen
Run both systems. Each practice agreed a sunset date for legacy, enforced by feature flag: enable the new system, monitor legacy usage, disable creation of new legacy tasks while still allowing updates to existing ones, then disable legacy editing altogether.
Benefit / cost
Migration risk came off the critical path entirely. In exchange we took on dual support and had to actively nudge practices off the old system.
This is a controlled-coexistence strategy, not migration avoidance — say it that way. The graduated wind-down is confirmed and worth emphasizing: creation disabled first with edits to existing tasks still allowed (no one's in-flight work breaks), then editing disabled altogether. That two-step is what made the sunset humane. PREP: "did every practice complete the sunset?" Know the true end state; if it was mid-rollout when you left, say so. Also: "why not migrate asynchronously after launch?", "did old and new tasks share an inbox?", "how did users know which to use?" — have real answers or honest ones.

Decision 2 of 3

Temporal tables made audit easy and history hard

  • Why they looked right: built-in row versioning, near-zero implementation cost, strong audit support, a familiar SQL Server capability
  • The failure mode: one user action (reassign, add a recipient, change status, post a message) lands as independent version rows in four tables. Building product history meant merging four version streams into one ordered, human-readable timeline, regenerated every time a task detail was viewed. The user-facing history was itself complex and dynamic, which made deriving it from temporal tables particularly difficult.
  • What I'd build today: temporal tables for compliance and forensics, plus a separate append-only activity log, written at mutation time, for user-facing history

The lesson: audit history and product-facing activity history have different consumers and different semantics, so one storage mechanism shouldn't serve both.

The deck's central technical lesson — take time here. Walk the concrete scenario slowly; it's what makes the lesson land. Confirmed details that strengthen the story: history was never materialized, it was regenerated on every task-detail view, and the MVP did that assembly on the FRONTEND from the raw version streams; doing it properly on the backend was one of the agreed post-MVP fixes (slide 4). Together these state the cost profile plainly: the temporal choice turned every task open into a four-stream merge. The append-only activity log you'd build today inverts that tradeoff (write-once, read-cheap), which is the crisp way to close the lesson. PREP: be ready for "was timestamp ordering sufficient?", "how did you attribute row changes to user-facing events?", "how did pagination of history perform?" — reconstruct as much as you can from memory beforehand.

Decision 3 of 3

Keeping task state on the server

  • Mutations completed on the server, then the affected views refreshed. The history panel, for example, refreshed on every edit.
  • There were a variety of pre-existing footguns in how data was synced between Redux and the backend, and I wanted to ensure the MVP avoided them
  • The trade: modest extra network traffic bought simpler consistency semantics and far less mutation logic to get wrong under deadline. The main alternative was mirroring state into Redux, which we deliberately bypassed for task data.
  • The data-fetching layer we built for this became a reusable strategy that replaced error-prone existing patterns beyond our feature
Don't claim narrow query invalidation unless it's true — the honest version ("we refetched, and that was the point") defends fine. PREP: "how did two open sessions stay consistent?" ties back to the near-realtime mechanism TODO on slide 5; "was a server-state library like React Query available then?" — know the timeline answer. Loading/error handling: describe what the reusable fetching layer standardized, from memory.

Execution

A plan built to surface risk early

  • Ticketed and sequenced nearly the whole project up front in vertical slices, ordered so four or more engineers could work in parallel without blocking each other. Ownership areas were matched to each engineer's interests.
  • An auto-adjusting Gantt chart re-forecast the completion date as tickets closed: schedule risk surfaced weeks before it became unrecoverable, while estimates and scope kept moving underneath

Nothing shipped to everyone at once

What failed

What went badly

  • The temporal-table choice made user-facing history a tremendous headache, with a tail of downstream complexity
  • Embedding the feature in legacy Visual Basic pages, on top of error-prone existing data fetching, added constant drag. The reusable fetching layer started as a way to cope with that.
  • I delegated implementation ownership without first making the architectural decision boundary explicit; by the time I raised concerns, the engineer had already invested heavily in their design
The third bullet is the process failure, owned as mine — not a story about a difficult engineer. PREP before telling it live: the technical disagreement itself, why both approaches were reasonable, how it was evaluated and decided, and how the engineer kept meaningful ownership afterward. If those specifics won't come back to you, keep the bullet at this level and don't go deeper. Optional extra if asked for more failures: the role→user mapping service for group sends turned out to be broken and needed fixing along the way.

Lessons I still use

  • The value of unearthing unknowns and decisions early along with project planning and sequencing
  • Adjusting project presentation (especially timelines) based on the audience
  • How to approach technical disagreements with SWEs who are heavily invested in their solution
