Project retrospective · Flatiron Health

Replacing a legacy EHR tasking system under a contractual deadline

Led a team of 4–5 engineers (me included) over roughly 6–8 months. We delivered a high-risk replacement on time by constraining scope, running alongside the legacy system instead of migrating, and rolling out incrementally.

Andrew Ingraham · Senior Software Engineer, project lead

One-liner first: "Tasks is how staff at an oncology practice route work to each other inside the EHR — near-realtime messaging tied to patients and orders." The title already states the outcome; the deck's job is to defend how.

Stakes & constraints

Why this wasn't just a CRUD feature

  • One of the ten most requested product changes for a long stretch, and highly visible inside the company.
  • A legacy Tasking feature that was badly broken but still actively used: the replacement had to be a functional superset, transitioned without breaking live clinical workflows.
  • A contractual obligation to deliver to a subset of practices by a fixed date, with significant business cost to a miss.
  • 3–4 engineers besides me, in one of two work-streams. The other stream was carrying an EHR certification with its own immovable deadline, so people had to stay movable between the two.
Every design decision that follows traces back to these four constraints; name that explicitly. PREP: "what was the business cost of missing?" — only claim what you actually know about the contract terms.

Scope

Where we drew the MVP line

MVP

  • Near-realtime messaging between users in a practice
  • Full task fields: subject, description, status, urgency, due date, assignee, recipients
  • Task history, for both user-friendly display and auditing
  • Notifications when a task is received
  • Inbox with pagination, sorting, and filtering
  • Patient and order associations, surfaced on those entities
  • Group sends (e.g. Nursing)

Permitted to come after MVP

  • Bulk task edits
  • Integrated frontend state, instead of refetching on change
  • History generation done properly on the backend (the MVP assembled it on the frontend)
  • Table indexes
  • In-app notifications
  • Styling improvements, of which there were many

PREP: "why is Notifications a separate table from Recipients?" — the exact schema is uncertain; the plausible lifecycle given the design: a row inserted per recipient when a task (or message) arrives, row-existence meaning "unread," cleared on view — with the temporal table preserving even deleted rows for audit. Verify against memory before presenting it as fact; if it stays fuzzy, say "the notification schema I don't recall precisely" and pivot to what you do know. "What happens to tasks when a user is deactivated?" — confirmed: a cronjob swept for old and orphaned tasks; a task could be orphaned when everyone associated with it had left the organization. Good edge-case story: it shows the design anticipated organizational churn, not just happy-path messaging. Concurrency (confirmed): multi-table writes wrapped in one transaction (no partial states), and optimistic concurrency on edits — the update failed if the task had changed since it was read, and the client refetched, which is the same code path as refresh-on-change. The transaction gave atomicity; the stale-read rejection prevented lost updates — name them as solving different problems. Messages were append-only, so the thread had no edit contention. Still unremembered: the token (rowversion vs. updatedAt) — "I don't recall which" is fine — and whether notification inserts were deduped by a unique (taskId, recipientId) constraint.

Decision 1 of 3

Coexist with legacy, don't migrate

Alternatives
One-time migration of legacy tasks; or patch the legacy feature in place.
Chosen
Run both systems. Each practice agreed a sunset date for legacy, enforced by feature flag: enable the new system, monitor legacy usage, disable legacy creation, then turn it off.
Benefit / cost
Migration risk came off the critical path entirely. In exchange we took on dual support and had to actively nudge practices off the old system.
Revisit trigger
If sunset dates had slipped en masse, or dual support had begun eating the team, we'd have revisited a one-time conversion.
This is a controlled-coexistence strategy, not migration avoidance — say it that way. PREP: "did the sunset actually complete?" Know the true end state; if it was mid-rollout when you left, say so. Also: "why not migrate asynchronously after launch?", "did old and new tasks share an inbox?", "how did users know which to use?" — have real answers or honest ones.

Decision 2 of 3 · the one that bit us

Temporal tables made audit easy and history hard

  • Why they looked right: built-in row versioning, near-zero implementation cost, strong audit support, a familiar SQL Server capability.
  • The failure mode: one user action (reassign, add a recipient, change status, post a message) lands as independent version rows in four tables. Building product history meant merging four version streams into one ordered, human-readable timeline. confirm the specifics: ordering approach, worst downstream bugs
  • What I'd build today: temporal tables for compliance and forensics, plus a separate append-only activity log, written at mutation time, for user-facing history.

The lesson: audit history and product-facing activity history have different consumers and different semantics, so one storage mechanism shouldn't serve both.

The deck's central technical lesson — take time here. Walk the concrete scenario slowly; it's what makes the lesson land. Confirmed detail that strengthens the story: the MVP assembled the history timeline on the FRONTEND from the raw version streams, and doing it properly on the backend was one of the agreed post-MVP fixes (slide 4). That's a vivid way to show the cost the temporal choice pushed downstream. PREP: be ready for "was timestamp ordering sufficient?", "how did you attribute row changes to user-facing events?", "how did pagination of history perform?" — reconstruct as much as you can from memory beforehand.

Decision 3 of 3

Keeping task state on the server

  • Mutations completed on the server, then the affected views refreshed. The history panel, for example, refreshed on every edit. confirm refresh scope: whole inbox or affected resources?
  • The trade: modest extra network traffic bought simpler consistency semantics and far less mutation logic to get wrong under deadline. The main alternative was mirroring state into Redux, which we deliberately bypassed for task data.
  • The data-fetching layer we built for this became a reusable strategy that replaced error-prone existing patterns beyond our feature.
Don't claim narrow query invalidation unless it's true — the honest version ("we refetched, and that was the point") defends fine. PREP: "how did two open sessions stay consistent?" ties back to the near-realtime mechanism TODO on slide 5; "was a server-state library like React Query available then?" — know the timeline answer. Loading/error handling: describe what the reusable fetching layer standardized, from memory.

Execution

A plan built to surface risk early

  • Ticketed and sequenced nearly the whole project up front in vertical slices, ordered so four or more engineers could work in parallel without blocking each other. Ownership areas were matched to each engineer's interests.
  • An auto-adjusting Gantt chart re-forecast the completion date as tickets closed: schedule risk surfaced weeks before it became unrecoverable, while estimates and scope kept moving underneath.

What failed

What went badly

  • The temporal-table choice made user-facing history a tremendous headache, with a tail of downstream complexity.
  • Embedding the feature in legacy Visual Basic pages, on top of error-prone existing data fetching, added constant drag. The reusable fetching layer started as a way to cope with that.
  • I delegated implementation ownership without first making the architectural decision boundary explicit; by the time I raised concerns, the engineer had already invested heavily in their design.
The third bullet is the process failure, owned as mine — not a story about a difficult engineer. PREP before telling it live: the technical disagreement itself, why both approaches were reasonable, how it was evaluated and decided, and how the engineer kept meaningful ownership afterward. If those specifics won't come back to you, keep the bullet at this level and don't go deeper. Optional extra if asked for more failures: the role→user mapping service for group sends turned out to be broken and needed fixing along the way.

Reflection

Three lessons I still use

  1. Separate audit storage from user-facing activity history. They have different consumers and need different designs.
  2. Define architectural decision rights before delegating implementation ownership, so disagreement surfaces during design review instead of after someone has sunk weeks into an approach.
  3. Sequence the whole project up front to expose the critical path, then keep revising scope and estimates against it as reality comes in.

Before starting, I interviewed several senior engineers about how they decomposed large projects, and synthesized that into the planning and design process I used here and have since taught to other teams.
