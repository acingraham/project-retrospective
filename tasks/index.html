Project retrospective

Tasks

Flatiron Health · your dates, e.g. 2022–2023

Andrew Ingraham · Senior Software Engineer, project lead

One-liner before touching the slides: "Tasks is how staff at an oncology practice route work to each other inside the EHR — near-realtime messaging tied to patients and orders, so nothing falls through the cracks between a visit and the next step."

Context

Where Tasks sits

  • Flatiron Health builds software for community oncology, including OncoEMR — the electronic health record these practices run on.
  • Tasks lets practice staff message each other in near-realtime about work that needs doing, with an inbox, notifications, and group sends to teams like Nursing.
  • A task can optionally be attached to an entity like a patient or an order, and then surfaces wherever that entity appears in the EHR.
45 seconds max. If asked "why does an EHR need this": clinical work is handoffs — a physician flags something, nursing follows up, front desk schedules. Before Tasks that coordination lived in workarounds outside the system of record. Fields, if asked: subject, description, status, urgency, due date, assignee, recipients.

The problem

Practices kept asking for this — and the tool they had was broken

  • In-EHR tasking sat in our top 10 most requested changes for an extended period.
  • A legacy Tasking feature existed but was badly broken — yet some practices still depended on it, so we couldn't just remove it. Anything new had to be a functional superset.
  • Why now: we were contractually committed to deliver to a subset of practices by a fixed date.
Likely follow-ups: "How did you know it was the right problem?" — sustained top-10 demand plus a contractual commitment made prioritization unusually clear; the real judgment calls were in scoping, not selection. "What did you deprioritize?" — answer with the concrete MVP cuts (prep on slide 5). PREP: "what exactly was broken about legacy Tasking?" needs 2–3 specific failure modes from memory, and how the new design addressed each — this also underpins build-over-patch. Only claim to know the contractual miss cost if you actually do.

My role

What I owned

Me

  • Led the project end to end: system design, scoping, sequencing the work, delivery, and rollout.
  • One of my first big projects leading multiple engineers — I went into it deliberately collecting approaches from senior engineers.

The team

  • A team of ~8 engineers split into two loose work-streams, so people could be reallocated as needed. I led the Tasks work-stream: 3–4 engineers besides me.
  • The catch: the other work-stream was an EHR certification with its own immovable deadline, so in practice people were hard to move.
Be precise: "I" for design, sequencing, and the calls on the next slides; "we" for the build. If asked about the work-stream tension: both deadlines were contractual or regulatory, so reallocation was a last resort — which raised the price of any slip and pushed me toward the risk-minimizing choices that follow. PREP: "where was product on a contractual, customer-facing feature?" — name your actual counterparts and the split (technical scoping and sequencing vs. customer commitments and requirements). Volunteering the partnership strengthens the story.

Approach

The bets we made

  1. Cut to a genuine MVP. Every scope decision optimized for the contractual date, cut in a way that wasted no effort so features could layer on cleanly after.
  2. Don't migrate legacy tasks. Run old and new side by side, and sunset legacy per practice behind a feature flag.
  3. Keep it boring. Plain use of the existing stack (C#/VB, React, SQL Server) and refresh-on-change instead of clever frontend state. Four tables — Tasks, Messages, Recipients, Notifications — each with a temporal table behind it for history and audit.
Each bet has a road-not-taken to voice if asked: we could have patched the legacy feature (broken foundation, still bounded by it); we could have migrated data (next slide); we could have built rich Redux state (speed and bug-avoidance won). PREP, near-certain questions: (1) "What did you actually cut?" — have 2–3 concrete MVP cuts by name, one tempting cut you kept, and the rule used to decide. "We cut what was non-essential" is circular and will not survive. (2) "If the frontend just refreshes, what makes it near-realtime?" — know the delivery mechanism (polling interval? push?) and typical latency; if you no longer recall the exact interval, say so plainly.

A decision worth discussing

Migrate the old tasks, or run both systems?

The options

  • Migrate — one system, clean cutover, no dual support burden.
  • Run both — no risky transformation of a broken feature's data, and we ship sooner.

What we chose, and why

We ran both. Each practice agreed an end date for legacy Tasks, which we could enforce via feature flag. The cost: supporting two systems at once and actively nudging practices off the old one — but it kept migration risk entirely off the critical path to the contractual date.

End with a pause — "happy to go deeper on this one." Strong follow-up material: the sunset lever mattered because "temporary" dual systems become permanent without an enforcement mechanism; the per-practice flag made the end date real rather than aspirational. PREP: "so did you actually turn the old one off?" comes first. Know the true end state — how far the sunset had progressed when you left. If it was mid-rollout, say that plainly; don't imply completion.

What got hard

Three things were harder than planned

  • Temporal tables made auditing nearly free, but reconstructing a user-friendly task history across four temporally-versioned tables was a tremendous headache, with a tail of downstream issues.
  • Embedding the feature across the app meant landing React inside legacy Visual Basic pages, on top of pre-existing data fetching that was error-prone. We built a reusable data-fetching strategy that replaced the old patterns beyond our own feature.
  • An engineer I'd given ownership to was committed to a design I disagreed with — navigating that without undermining the ownership I'd granted was its own project.
No villains — describe constraints, not people. On the disagreement: the tension was between honoring delegated ownership and holding the system design line; how I'd handle it now is on the last slide. PREP, guaranteed follow-up: "how did it end?" Have the concrete resolution ready in under a minute — what you actually did, what shipped, and the working relationship after. PREP, technical deep-dive: be ready to explain *why* history was hard (stitching one human-readable timeline out of four separate version streams) and what you'd use instead — that design is the slide-10 claim. Optional extra example if asked for more: the service mapping roles to users (for group sends like "Nursing") turned out to be broken and needed fixing along the way.

Execution

How we ran it

  • Ticketed and sequenced essentially the entire project up front — vertical slices, ordered so 4+ engineers could work in parallel without gating each other. An auto-adjusting Gantt chart tracked our estimated completion date against the deadline continuously.
  • Matched engineers' interests to areas of ownership.
  • Rolled out incrementally behind a feature flag: lower-risk practices who wanted early access first, then GA. Bug bashes with stakeholders and pre-mortems ahead of go-live.
The upfront sequencing is the thing interviewers bite on: the point wasn't a waterfall plan, it was making parallelism safe — knowing at any moment whether the date was at risk, weeks before it became unrecoverable. The Gantt chart re-estimated as tickets closed, so "are we on track?" was a lookup, not a meeting. PREP: "sounds like waterfall — tell me about a time the plan was wrong." Have one concrete example of the plan diverging and being corrected (a blown estimate, a resequencing, scope moving mid-flight); without it the reframe reads as theory.

Retrospective

What I'd do differently

  • Temporal tables were right for audit but wrong as the backbone of user-facing history. I'd prototype the hardest read path — "show me this task's history" — before committing the schema, instead of discovering its cost downstream.
  • On delegation: I'd establish how design decisions get made before handing over ownership, so disagreement surfaces as design review rather than as a standoff after someone is committed.
Before using these live, confirm each remedy is one you'd genuinely claim as your practice now, and have a real example of applying it on a later project — "have you actually done that since?" is the standard probe. If a remedy isn't true for you, keep the diagnosis (which is yours) and drop the prescription.

Retrospective

What I took from it

  • My approach to system design on a team. I asked 4–5 senior engineers how they approached it; one shared a strategy I still follow — and have since passed on to multiple teams.
  • Adjusting communication style to the audience — executives, practices, and engineers each needed a different version of the same status.
  • How to work with an engineer who's committed to an approach I disagree with, after I've given them the ownership.
